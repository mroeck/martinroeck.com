My research addresses a gap at the centre of climate policy for buildings. For
decades, efforts to reduce emissions from construction focused almost entirely
on operational energy — heating, cooling, electricity. As buildings have become
more efficient to run, the emissions embodied in their materials and
construction have grown into the larger share of the problem, and in many cases
the part that is irreversible: once a building is built, that carbon is already
in the atmosphere.

I work on **how to measure those emissions reliably, how to set credible
targets for them, and what actually brings them down** — at the scale of
individual buildings, national building stocks, and the global construction
sector.

Doing this well requires both rigour and reach. Life cycle assessment is a
precise method applied, in practice, to extremely patchy data. Much of my work
is therefore as much about building the data infrastructure the field needs as
about any single finding. I collaborate across disciplines — with materials
scientists, civil and architectural engineers, industrial ecologists,
integrated assessment modellers, economists and policy analysts — and with the
people who have to use the results.

Methodologically, I combine life cycle sustainability assessment with building
information modelling, building data science and scenario modelling. Recurring
tools include bottom-up building stock models, statistical analysis of
environmental product declarations and building LCA case data, archetype-based
stock characterisation, and open-source modelling in Python.

In the following, a brief overview of my main research foci:

- **Embodied and whole life carbon of buildings.** The thread running through
  most of my work: quantifying greenhouse gas emissions across the full life
  cycle of buildings, understanding what drives them, and identifying the
  design, material and policy choices that reduce them. This began with
  comparative analysis of building LCA case studies and continues through
  reduction and removal strategies at building and stock level.

- **Benchmarks, baselines and targets.** Measurement is only useful if there is
  something to measure against. I work on deriving robust reference values from
  real building data, and on connecting them to science-based carbon budgets —
  bridging what the building sector currently emits and what it can emit. This
  runs from the European embodied carbon benchmarking work through INDICATE to
  the GLOBE benchmarking taskforce.

- **Building data infrastructure and data science.** Most of the above fails
  without data. I develop open building data models, datasets and tools that
  let life cycle analysis be done at scale and reproducibly — including SLiCE,
  the Embodied Carbon of European Buildings database, the global buildings
  database behind GBDI, and the WLC Scenario Explorer.

- **Building stock scenarios and carbon removals.** Modelling how national and
  global building stocks evolve, and what different construction, renovation
  and material pathways mean for emissions to 2050 — including the contested
  question of how much carbon dioxide can genuinely be stored in buildings
  through timber, bio-based materials and mineral carbonation.

- **Regenerative spatial systems.** The longer-term question behind the carbon
  accounting: what it would mean for the way we build and inhabit space to
  restore rather than deplete the systems it depends on. This is where my work
  on sufficiency, participatory housing and social innovation sits.

- **Science for policy.** Translating all of this into instruments decision
  makers can use. I have led the analytical work behind European policy
  processes including the whole life carbon roadmap for DG ENV and the analysis
  of life cycle emissions of EU buildings and construction for DG GROW, and
  advise national governments and international organisations.
